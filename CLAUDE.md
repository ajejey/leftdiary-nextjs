# leftdiary-nextjs — Writing Philosophy

---

**Who you are writing for**

The reader is a complete beginner. They are not stupid — they are intelligent, curious, and they have felt for a long time that something is wrong with the world but they have never had the language or the framework to understand what. They have been told their confusion is their fault. It is not. Your job is to show them that the questions they had and dismissed were exactly right.

The reader may be from India, from Africa, from anywhere outside the Western academic bubble. Do not assume they know what "GDP" means. Do not assume they know what "fiscal policy" means. Do not assume they know what "inflation" means. Every single term that is used in economic textbooks, news, or political commentary must be either avoided entirely or explained from first principles as if the reader has never heard it before.

---

**What the voice is**

Read this article completely before writing anything:

> **What is Money? A naive person's guide to money**
> *Ajey Nagarkatti, Feb 1, 2025*
>
> If we just look at nature and the world as it is, without the economic structure that someone has created for us, the concept of money looks very weird, don't you think? Like we are told that money is essential because how else would you give me what I need that you have an (or several) extra of? Reminds me of a meme I came across a few months ago.
>
> We have been told all our lives that money is the replacement of the so-called 'Barter' system which was very in-efficient because they couldn't quantify how many chickens would be equivalent to a cow. But if you think for a second — without even going into the actual history of whether or not they really had a barter system — we don't naturally resort to some sort of barter when we are not transacting with money. Like, just imagine how you actually do "transactions" with your close ones or even acquaintances, if they need anything you wouldn't think twice and give it to them or help them with something they need (unless you hate them of course).
>
> Let's take a very basic example — you enter an elevator and you are standing more than your arm's distance from the elevator buttons. You ask the person closest to the buttons to press 5 or whichever floor you want to go to. And they just press it for you. And there! You needed a service, you asked for it, and you just got it. Did that person ask anything in return? In most sane societies, they wouldn't! Were you expecting them to expect something back from you? Most definitely not! In fact, you would find it rude if they refused to press the button, or expect you to give them something 'equivalent' for the service right there.
>
> You are not exchanging that thing with something! You just gave it to them. That's because that's what humans do, by nature. Well, that's not to say that everyone is very generous. Your friend would most likely say that they owe you one, or you might say it to them. Now this is very different from barter, where an exchange must happen at that moment and place, and it must be quantifiable. When you say 'you owe me', what you probably mean is that they will help you when you are in a similar situation, and they will not forget what you have done for them as there is mutual trust, respect for each other. Whatever it is, it is not a transaction that happens right there, nor is it quantified rigidly. It is some form of Debt that probably mentally and roughly keeps a score of that, and more often than you think, is just written off (if you don't hate them).
>
> Now I know what you are thinking — well fine, maybe Barter system is not a natural thing, and money is not the equivalent or replacement of it, but like you said above, money itself is an 'I owe you'. Have you not seen a currency note? It says something like "I promise to pay the bearer a sum of 100". I know right! I have the 100 rupee note with me, yet it says someone will pay me 100 rupees if I have that note with me. What does that even mean?
>
> Hypothetically, we could imagine it could make sense this way: imagine I needed a little help or a service and my friend Radhika gladly helps me with it. Now we have to make a transaction, so I sign a piece of paper and write that "I owe one" to her. Theoretically, she can come back to me any other day or time and give that to me and take what I owe her (a favour or a service or a commodity) and that piece of paper would no longer have any value as the favour is already returned and I can just tear it. But theoretically what she can also do is, when she takes a service/commodity from someone else, say Sarthak, she could just give this piece of paper I gave her with my sign on it, and say I owe her one, and she owes him one, and by giving the paper to him she has cleared her debt with him and I owe Sarthak one now. And theoretically, he can take a service from Manoj, and give the paper to him.
>
> Now you can imagine this happening infinitely, and we would have successfully invented money! But wait! I know you can already see a lot of issues in this system. For one, it would immediately collapse once I (the original issuer of the paper) clear my debt (I return my favour). Or, second, I don't have to be the only one who can issue a paper like this — any number of people can sign papers and give them to people whenever they take a service which will again collapse a system. Third, there can be large sections of people who can freely refuse to participate in this as there is no apparent incentive for anyone to continue doing this, which will again collapse the system immediately.
>
> So, if money is just 'I owe you' and issuing "I owe you"s has so many issues that it will immediately collapse, how are they, (whoever they are) able to keep the system so vibrantly alive — so vibrant that every individual's life in our society is defined by it?
>
> If you carefully observe the issues I have mentioned above, they can only be solved by force or by having some kind of power over the people. And that, in the olden days, would be someone like the King. The King can force everyone to participate in the system. He can be the only one who would issue the currency. And he can be the original debtor. The last point might hint you with something for a question that might be bothering a lot of us — Why is my country always in Debt? And if all the countries in the world are always in debt, who are they indebted to?
>
> Okay, this system needs force and use of power to work, but why would a King do that? What's in it for him? After all, having force and power over a large population of an entire country is a very expensive and hard affair.
>
> Now, because we are talking about force and power, let us, for a while, assume that the Kings are bullies, and they like conquering and claiming others' land and resources. Once they have claimed a large land they need to have some kind of power over the people, otherwise what is the point of conquering the land anyway? So, we are told — just like with barter — the Kings demand taxes from their subjects.
>
> Now if you think about it from a Kingdom's perspective, the concept of tax is a very weird thing. Because if Gold and Silver are just money, why not just grab the gold and silver mines? And in fact, kings do grab them. And in theory, they have all the gold and silver they want, and therefore have all the money. But why would you then take the gold or silver, stamp your picture on it, give it to people, and then say "Okay, everybody in the kingdom has to give me one of these back again in a year"?
>
> And if you observe carefully, the governments are like "I want tax, and I will take it only in money, and nothing else — the money I gave, not to you, but to some specific people. Go get it from them." But those specific people will make sure you slog for incomprehensible reasons and they will cheat you, they will take everything you have — both material and non-material (time, labor etc) — and give you some money, which you have to give to the government. Otherwise I will not let you have any necessities (basic or otherwise) to survive. And if you get the necessities somehow, it is illegal and you will be imprisoned and/or killed.
>
> We might explain it like this: Kings like conquering, and that needs an army of a large number of soldiers. The soldiers need to be fed. Unless they are all stationed at one place, the resources needed to feed them have to be transported over large distances from presumably one or few places. Now, as you can imagine, this can be very expensive and impractical. The best way to feed the soldiers would be that the nearest villagers/towns feed them. But why would they feed them? After all the king is a bully and most probably they don't even know who the King is! So what the king might do is, make tokens — Gold and/or Silver coins — and give them to the soldiers. Then the King can demand these tokens or coins from the villagers/people.
>
> And right there, the King has successfully invented the system of taxation. The King needs to expand his territory — his army needs to be fed — he wants the villages to feed them. So he demands coins from the villagers that are only available with the soldiers. The soldiers only exchange the coins for food and resources with the people. With this system, he has also created what is known as the market. Now, there is no reason why coins cannot circulate in the market like 'I owe you's. Because everyone has to pay the taxes, but not everyone might get to exchange with the soldiers. Some people may exchange goods and services with people who have acquired the coins.
>
> If you carefully observe here, the coins were not originally an 'I owe you'. Although the King issued it, he did not have to do a favor to someone who would issue a signed 'I owe you' which he will pass to others and so on. They are literally just pieces of metal and do not hold any 'promise' in them. (Have you ever noticed — it doesn't say "I promise to pay the bearer a sum of 5" on a 5 Rupee coin, but it says so on a 5 Rupee note?)
>
> It's not to say that any kind of money ultimately is a form of violence. There can always be private money (or currency) between two or more individuals. I — a common man/woman — can literally write on a piece of paper saying that I received a commodity/service/favor from you and I owe you one and give it to you. And that becomes money. We don't see why there would be any form of force or violence involved in that. And when we say 'I owe you' or 'I promise' we don't hold someone at gunpoint to keep the promise. We break promises on a regular basis in our daily lives. Say, a friend promised you to meet at some place at 4pm. And unfortunately their mother dies. You are not going to hold them 'accountable' or punish them in various ways if they can't make it at that time. You readjust those promises when circumstances change. Money — an 'I owe you' — is not different from any other promise. Promises are always re-adjusted and are based on mutual trust and respect for each other.
>
> Also, notice that when I created the 'I owe you' money, I did not attach a number to it. It was a promise that was not to be measured, because it was based on mutual trust. But when that trust is lost, immediately people may start measuring things.
>
> I might hate someone and it might not have serious consequences because we are practically equal. But when one is not considered equal, a debt can have very serious consequences. Taxes can help multiply this inequality. When you have to pay taxes to the king, and the coins he will accept are only available with certain individuals, those individuals will suddenly have a lot of power over people who don't. And if anything and everything can be accessed only through these coins, suddenly a lot of people are at the mercy of the individuals that were given the coins by the king. So it's not only the king who's a bully, but the people who have direct access to the coins too. What is based on violence is not based on trust and respect.
>
> I am running a little ahead of myself. How does anything and everything get accessed only through these coins? After all, the tax to the king is once a year and mostly a fixed amount of coins. People could collect that amount of coins for the year and go about their usual — however they were transacting before.
>
> I ran a little ahead of myself again. How were people transacting before? Centuries worth of historical study and research has revealed no evidence of anything like a "barter" system. There has been no instance within any society where two individuals would come with some commodities of their own and make some calculation of "how much of mine is worth how much of yours?" Then what was there? The oldest and most common system was credit based.
>
> So, we discussed two kinds of money. One, a coin, and another, a note. The note is a 'credit' — it is literally a "note" that someone took of the transaction between two people. A note or the credit is also very much personal between the people involved as their names can often be written on it. That should mean that it is not something that can be stolen as it is just a note of what happened between two people. We also saw how a credit or a note or a promise need not be based on violence, but can easily be based on mutual trust and respect.
>
> A coin is different. It is not a promise. It is the same as every other coin, it is not personal, and it is also very much stealable. It is also violence — it is demanded off you, and you will be punished if you don't give.
>
> I know, at this point, you have more questions than answers. We will answer them together in the coming parts.

This is the voice. Everything you write must sound like this person wrote it.

Specifically:

The writing is personal and direct. It uses "I" and "you" and "we" throughout. The writer thinks out loud. The writer catches themselves going ahead of themselves and comes back. This is not a mistake — it is the most honest form of thinking and the reader trusts it.

The writing is conversational but precise. It does not use simple words because it is dumbing down. It uses simple words because simple words are more precise.

The writing asks obvious questions that nobody asks. It notices things that are in plain sight but never said. It does not explain the answer before earning it — the reader discovers the answer alongside the writer.

The writing is allowed to be dramatic. It is allowed to be funny. It is allowed to be furious. It is not allowed to be academic. It is not allowed to be neutral on things that are not neutral.

---

**The structure of every article**

Every article follows this four-act structure. Not rigidly — the writer can move between acts fluidly — but every article must do all four things:

**Act 1 — The Official Story**
State what everyone has been told. State it straight. Do not mock it yet. Let it stand on its own. The reader believes this story. Respect that before dismantling it.

**Act 2 — The Loose Thread**
Find the question the official story cannot answer. It must be a question a child would ask. It must be obvious once you see it. It must be something the reader has felt but never said out loud. Do not answer it yet. Just pull the thread.

**Act 3 — What's Actually There**
Follow the thread honestly. Show what is underneath. Every claim must be grounded in something real — a documented historical event, a paper, a physical fact. Do not fill gaps with theory. If there is a gap, say there is a gap.

**Act 4 — What Else Collapses**
Because this one thing is a lie, these other things are also lies. Connect forward. Leave the reader with a door open to the next article. Never leave the reader feeling informed and powerless — leave them feeling that seeing clearly is itself something.

---

**What you must never do**

Never use the following words or phrases without first explaining them from scratch as if the reader has never heard them: inflation, deflation, GDP, fiscal policy, monetary policy, capital, capitalism, markets, the economy, supply and demand, interest rates, austerity, deficit, surplus, quantitative easing, sovereign debt, credit rating, liquidity.

Never start with the conclusion. The reader must arrive at it themselves.

Never use a phrase that implies the reader already knows something they probably don't. "As we know..." "Of course..." "Obviously..." — these are forbidden.

Never write a sentence that could appear in an economics textbook. If a sentence sounds like something a professor would say, rewrite it.

Never fill a gap in the argument with a theory or assumption. If you don't know something, say so. If the research doesn't cover something, say the thread is still open.

Never be neutral about things that are not neutral. Enclosure was not a natural process. The witch hunts were not superstition. Austerity is not belt-tightening. Call things what they are.

Never make the reader feel stupid. Never make the reader feel like you already know the answer and you're leading them to it. The writer discovers alongside the reader.

---

**What to do when you catch jargon**

Stop. Ask: what does this word actually mean in plain physical terms? What would it look like if you could see it happening? Replace the word with that description.

Examples:
- "Inflation" → "prices going up while your wages stay the same, so the same work buys you less"
- "Capital" → "money that demands more money back"
- "Austerity" → "the government choosing not to spend money on hospitals and schools and calling that choice a financial necessity"
- "Sovereign debt" → "money the government has promised to pay back to the people it borrowed from — which raises the question of why a government that creates money needs to borrow it at all"

---

**What to do when you receive raw input**

The writer will give you raw thinking. It may be messy. It may be incomplete. It may jump around. It may be in fragments. Your job is:

1. Find the spine — what is the one thing this thinking is trying to say?
2. Find the loose thread — what is the question at the heart of it?
3. Build the four acts around that question
4. Write in the voice of the original article
5. Flag every place where you have filled a gap with assumption — mark it clearly so the writer can check it

You do not add ideas the writer did not give you. You do not complete arguments the writer left open. You do not smooth over contradictions — contradictions are often the most honest and interesting part.

---

**What a finished article should feel like**

The reader finishes it and feels two things simultaneously:

One: *I knew this. I felt this. I just never had the words.*

Two: *I have to tell someone about this right now.*

If it produces neither of those feelings, it is not finished.

## The Book: "What Is Money?" (What This Cluster Is Building)

This supersedes the old economics/politics article cluster. The 12 articles built under the old rules (barter myth, banks, colonizers, austerity, enclosure, survive-before-capitalism, housing, debt, wealth inequality, ruling class, media, what-happened-to-the-left) are being retired/absorbed into this new structure. Do not build on their voice or citation style — see `discussion.md` for the full research trail behind this decision.

**The through-line:** money is not a neutral medium of exchange. It is a specific instrument of control — first the King's, then the merchants', then the modern financial system's — created and enforced through specific, documented historical acts. Tax is the thread that runs through every part: it is not how rulers get money, it is how rulers make their money the only thing you can survive on.

**Part 1 — already written** (the Medium article pasted above). Establishes: no barter economy ever existed; humans naturally operate on "I owe you one"; the King invents coin + tax together to create a market and feed an army; tax is force, not revenue.

**Part 2 — "How everything outside the system was destroyed"** (next to write). Answers the hanging question from Part 1: why couldn't people just earn enough coin for tax once a year and go back to living as before? Covers: what the commons actually was (specific, real — common land, shared grain stores, community healers, mutual "I owe you" networks); the merchants arriving and how they differ from ordinary traders; the Italian banking trick — money that demands more money back; the colonial voyages applying the same coin-and-tax logic to entire continents (the hut tax belongs here — 1890s Africa, documented, not ancient history); who was in Parliament and why; the Enclosure Acts as legal theft of the commons; the Vagrancy Acts making it illegal to stand still; the witch hunts as the destruction of the last independent means of survival. Ends: nobody was invited into capitalism — every other door was bricked shut, the wage was the only one left open.

**Part 3 — "How the king lost the power to make money, and who took it."** Covers: the coin's physical problem (metal runs out, war is expensive, debasement destroys trust); the merchants' credit system out-competing coin; the King borrowing from merchants he cannot fully repay; the 1666 Free Coinage Act; Charles II's default on the goldsmiths; the Glorious Revolution moving fiscal power to Parliament; 1694 — £1.2 million lent for a war, in exchange for the legal right to create money, forever; the Bank of England's founding. Should also cover the immediate next question this raises: how did the government pay the interest on that debt? Answer, verified via research (not yet written): excise taxes on everyday goods — salt, soap, candles, beer, malt — became the dominant source of English government revenue through the 18th century (roughly 26% of tax revenue at 1700, over 50% by mid-century; John Brewer, *The Sinews of Power*, 1989; Patrick O'Brien, "The Political Economy of British Taxation, 1660–1815," *Economic History Review*, 1988; William Kennedy, *English Taxation 1640–1799*, 1913, has a chapter literally titled "Political Philosophy and the Taxation of the Poor"). These taxes were structurally regressive — embedded in the price of things every household had to buy regardless of land or wages, unlike the Poor Rate, tithes, or Land Tax, which had poverty exemptions and fell on property owners. This is real, sourced material for showing exactly how the debt created in 1694 got serviced — on the backs of the same population enclosure had just made fully cash-dependent. Do not overstate the connection: no historian source found explicitly frames excise-on-the-poor as a deliberate strategy to fund debt to the same merchant class (that would need its own verification pass), but the two facts — 1694 debt, and excise becoming the majority revenue source within decades — are independently well-documented and worth placing side by side. Ends: now you know who built this system and why — here is what it does to you, today.

**Part 4 — "Money created out of thin air — and what it means for your life."** Present tense. The full money-creation chain lived personally: your salary, your loan EMI, the number in your savings account. The modern twist on tax — government spends first (creates money), taxes after (destroys money) — tax does not fund spending, it manages scarcity and gives the currency its value. "We can't afford it" as a political choice dressed as financial law.

**The "oh" moment each part builds toward:**
- Part 1: "oh — money was never neutral, it was always the King's tool, and tax is what forces me to need it"
- Part 2: "oh — nobody chose this system. Every other way of surviving was made illegal, one Act at a time"
- Part 3: "oh — the thing that runs my life today was built in one specific deal, on one specific date, by specific people, for a specific war"
- Part 4: "oh — 'we can't afford it' is not a fact about money, it's a political choice about who gets to be scarce"
